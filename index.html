What is probability?

Probability is a number from 0 to 1 that measures how likely an event is. 0 means it cannot happen, 1 means it is certain, and 0.5 means a fair coin-flip chance. Percentages convert directly: 35% = 0.35 = 35/100.

Every probability lives on this line.

How probabilities are found

  • Classical (equally likely outcomes): P(E) = (outcomes in E) / (outcomes in S). Example: P(even) = 3/6 = 0.5. Dice, cards, committees.
  • Given directly: most Exam P problems hand you probabilities or percentages (“30% of policyholders…”) and ask you to combine them with the rules in this part.
  • Long-run frequency: the fraction of times an event happens over many repeats. This is the intuition behind both of the above.

1. Set theory and the probability axioms

Vocabulary

Sample space S is the set of all outcomes; an event is a subset of S. A∪B means “A or B (or both)”, A∩B means “A and B”, A′ is the complement (“not A”). Events are mutually exclusive if A∩B = ∅.

Axioms and rules

0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1, P(S) = 1
P(A′) = 1 − P(A)
P(A∪B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A∩B)
P(A∪B∪C) = ΣP(single) − ΣP(pairs) + P(A∩B∩C)
P(A∩B′) = P(A) − P(A∩B)
De Morgan: (A∪B)′ = A′∩B′, (A∩B)′ = A′∪B′
If A ⊂ B then P(A) ≤ P(B)

Why the rules work: everything comes from three axioms

An axiom is a rule we accept without proof. Probability needs only three. Every other formula on this page is built from them, and seeing how makes the formulas easy to rebuild if you forget one under pressure.

AxiomIn symbolsIn plain words
1. No negativesP(A) ≥ 0 for every event AA probability can never be below zero.
2. Something happensP(S) = 1Some outcome in the sample space is certain to occur.
3. Separate pieces addIf A∩B = ∅ then P(A∪B) = P(A) + P(B)If two events cannot happen together, the chance of “one or the other” is the sum. The same holds for any list of events that pairwise cannot overlap.
Derivation D1 · the complement rule P(A′) = 1 − P(A)
KNOWThe three axiomsWANTA formula for P(not A)
  1. A and A′ cannot both happen: an outcome is either in A or not.A ∩ A′ = ∅
  2. Together they cover every outcome.A ∪ A′ = S
  3. Because they don’t overlap, Axiom 3 lets us add.P(A ∪ A′) = P(A) + P(A′)
  4. But A ∪ A′ is S, and Axiom 2 says P(S) = 1.P(A) + P(A′) = 1
  5. Subtract P(A) from both sides.P(A′) = 1 − P(A)
  6. Bonus results: S′ = ∅, so P(∅) = 1 − 1 = 0. And since P(A′) ≥ 0 (Axiom 1), P(A) can never exceed 1.P(∅) = 0 and 0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1
Derivation D2 · the addition rule P(A∪B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A∩B)
KNOWAxiom 3 only works for non-overlapping piecesWANTP(A or B) when A and B can overlap
  1. Cut the two circles of the Venn diagram into three pieces that do not overlap: A only, both, B only.A∪B = (A∩B′) ∪ (A∩B) ∪ (A′∩B)
  2. Separate pieces add (Axiom 3).P(A∪B) = P(A∩B′) + P(A∩B) + P(A′∩B)
  3. Circle A on its own is two pieces: A only, plus both.P(A) = P(A∩B′) + P(A∩B)
  4. Circle B on its own is also two pieces.P(B) = P(A′∩B) + P(A∩B)
  5. Add those two lines. You get all three pieces, but the middle piece appears twice.P(A) + P(B) = [all three pieces] + P(A∩B) = P(A∪B) + P(A∩B)
  6. Move the extra copy of the middle to the other side.P(A∪B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A∩B)
  7. Line 3 rearranged also gives the “A but not B” rule for free.P(A∩B′) = P(A) − P(A∩B)
Derivation D3 · De Morgan’s laws
KNOWThe meaning of “or”, “and”, “not”WANT(A∪B)′ = A′∩B′ and (A∩B)′ = A′∪B′
  1. Say the first one in words. “Not (A or B)” means neither happened.not(A or B) = (not A) and (not B)
  2. Check it with the die: A = {1,2}, B = {2,3}. Then A∪B = {1,2,3}, and its complement is {4,5,6}.(A∪B)′ = {4,5,6}
  3. Compute the right-hand side: A′ = {3,4,5,6}, B′ = {1,4,5,6}. What they share is {4,5,6}.A′∩B′ = {4,5,6} ✓
  4. The second law in words: “not both” means at least one of them failed.not(A and B) = (not A) or (not B)
  5. Check: A∩B = {2}, so (A∩B)′ = {1,3,4,5,6}. And A′∪B′ = {3,4,5,6} ∪ {1,4,5,6} = {1,3,4,5,6}.(A∩B)′ = A′∪B′ ✓
  6. How you use it: “neither” problems become 1 minus the union.P(neither) = P(A′∩B′) = 1 − P(A∪B)
Derivation D4 · three-event inclusion–exclusion (why we add the triple back)
KNOWA Venn diagram of three events has 7 regions inside the circlesWANTP(A∪B∪C) = ΣP(single) − ΣP(pairs) + P(A∩B∩C)
  1. The goal: a formula that counts every region inside the circles exactly once. Test the formula on each type of region.Region types: in exactly 1 event, in exactly 2, in all 3
  2. A region in exactly one event (say A only) appears in P(A) once, in no pair, and not in the triple.1 − 0 + 0 = 1 ✓
  3. A region in exactly two events (say A and B, not C) appears in P(A) and P(B), so 2 times among singles. It appears in one pair, P(A∩B). It is not in the triple.2 − 1 + 0 = 1 ✓
  4. The centre region (in all three) appears in all 3 singles and all 3 pairs, and in the triple.3 − 3 + 1 = 1 ✓
  5. Without the “+ triple” term, the centre would be counted 3 − 3 = 0 times, which is why it has to be added back.Every region counted exactly once ⇒ formula correct
  6. The same counting trick proves the “exactly one” formula, Σsingles − 2Σpairs + 3·triple. Exactly-two region: 2 − 2(1) + 0 = 0. Centre: 3 − 2(3) + 3 = 0. Exactly-one region: 1. So only the exactly-one regions survive.P(exactly one) = ΣP(single) − 2ΣP(pairs) + 3P(A∩B∩C)
  7. And the “exactly two” formula, Σpairs − 3·triple. Exactly-two region: 1 − 0 = 1. Centre: 3 − 3 = 0.P(exactly two) = ΣP(pairs) − 3P(A∩B∩C)
Derivation D5 · how big or small can the overlap be?
KNOWP(A) = 0.7, P(B) = 0.6, nothing elseWANTThe possible range of P(A∩B)
  1. Largest possible overlap: the overlap sits inside both A and B, so it cannot be bigger than the smaller one.P(A∩B) ≤ min(P(A), P(B)) = min(0.7, 0.6) = 0.6
  2. Smallest possible overlap: the union cannot exceed 1. Rearrange the addition rule.P(A∩B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A∪B) ≥ P(A) + P(B) − 1
  3. Plug in the numbers.P(A∩B) ≥ 0.7 + 0.6 − 1 = 0.3
  4. So 0.3 ≤ P(A∩B) ≤ 0.6. Use this to throw out impossible answer choices instantly. If the lower bound is negative, it is just 0.max(0, P(A)+P(B)−1) ≤ P(A∩B) ≤ min(P(A), P(B))

Exactly-k events

For two events: P(exactly one) = P(A) + P(B) − 2P(A∩B). For three events: P(exactly one) = ΣP(single) − 2ΣP(pairs) + 3P(A∩B∩C); P(exactly two) = ΣP(pairs) − 3P(A∩B∩C); P(none) = 1 − P(A∪B∪C).

Technique: Venn diagram or table. Fill in the innermost region (all events) first and work outward by subtraction. With two events, a 2×2 table of A/A′ against B/B′ with margins makes most problems one-step.

3. Conditional probability, total probability and Bayes

P(A|B) = P(A∩B) / P(B), defined for P(B) > 0
P(A∩B) = P(A|B)·P(B) = P(B|A)·P(A) (multiplication rule)
P(A) = Σ P(A|Bᵢ)·P(Bᵢ) (law of total probability, Bᵢ a partition)
P(Bⱼ|A) = P(A|Bⱼ)P(Bⱼ) / Σ P(A|Bᵢ)P(Bᵢ) (Bayes’ theorem)

Useful extra: P(A′|B) = 1 − P(A|B), and the chain rule P(A∩B∩C) = P(A)·P(B|A)·P(C|A∩B).

Derivation D6 · how all four formulas grow from one definition
KNOWThe definition P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B)WANTThe multiplication rule, total probability and Bayes
  1. Why the definition makes sense: knowing B happened shrinks the sample space to B. The part of A that is still possible is A∩B. Rescale so the new world, B, has probability 1.P(A|B) = P(A∩B) / P(B)
  2. Multiply both sides by P(B). That gives the multiplication rule. Swapping the letters gives a second version.P(A∩B) = P(B)·P(A|B) = P(A)·P(B|A)
  3. Total probability: let B₁, B₂, B₃ be a partition (no overlaps, covering everything). A splits into the non-overlapping pieces A∩B₁, A∩B₂, A∩B₃.P(A) = P(A∩B₁) + P(A∩B₂) + P(A∩B₃)
  4. Rewrite each piece with the multiplication rule.P(A) = P(B₁)P(A|B₁) + P(B₂)P(A|B₂) + P(B₃)P(A|B₃)
  5. Bayes: start from the definition with the roles swapped.P(B₁|A) = P(B₁∩A) / P(A)
  6. Replace the top with the multiplication rule and the bottom with total probability.P(B₁|A) = P(B₁)P(A|B₁) / Σ P(Bᵢ)P(A|Bᵢ)
  7. So Bayes is nothing new. It is the definition of conditional probability, with the top and bottom each worked out by the rules above.One route ÷ all routes
  8. Conditional probabilities obey every ordinary rule inside the shrunken world, for example the complement and addition rules.P(A′|B) = 1 − P(A|B)P(A∪C | B) = P(A|B) + P(C|B) − P(A∩C | B)

  1. List the partition (e.g. risk classes) and their prior probabilities.
  2. Multiply each prior by its conditional probability of the observed event; these are the joint probabilities.
  3. Divide the one you want by the sum of all of them.

4. Independence

A and B are independent iff P(A∩B) = P(A)P(B), equivalently P(A|B) = P(A).
If A,B are independent, so are (A,B′), (A′,B) and (A′,B′).
Three events are mutually independent only if every pair and also the triple satisfy the product rule.
Independent ≠ mutually exclusive. If P(A)>0, P(B)>0 and A∩B=∅, then P(A∩B)=0 ≠ P(A)P(B), so exclusive events with positive probabilities are never independent.

Handy results. For independent events: P(A∪B) = 1 − P(A′)P(B′), and P(at least one of n independent events) = 1 − ∏(1 − pᵢ). Series and parallel systems are tested this way: a parallel system works if at least one component works.

⊥
Independent means “knowing one tells you nothing about the other.” If learning that B happened doesn’t change the chance of A, then P(A|B) = P(A). Put that into the multiplication rule P(A∩B) = P(B)·P(A|B) and you get the product rule P(A∩B) = P(A)·P(B). Independence is either told to you (“independently”, “at random with replacement”, separate people or machines) or tested with the product rule.

5. Exam strategy for this section

The five-step method for any Part 1 question

  1. Name the events with lettersOne letter per idea: A = auto, H = home, C = claim. Write them down; it stops you mixing them up halfway through.
  2. Label every number in the questionDecide whether each number is a single P(A), a joint P(A∩B), or a conditional P(A|B). The table below shows which words signal which. This step is where most mistakes happen.
  3. Write the target as a formula“Want P(H|C)” or “Want P(A′∩B′)”. Now you know what has to go in the numerator and the denominator.
  4. Choose the toolOverlapping categories with percentages → 2×2 table or Venn diagram. Groups with different rates → tree, total probability, Bayes. Equally likely outcomes → counting. “Independent” → multiply. “At least one” → 1 − P(none).
  5. Compute, then sanity-checkIs the answer between 0 and 1? Is a joint no bigger than either single probability? Do posteriors add to 1? Is a conditional at least as big as the matching joint?

“30% of policyholders have auto”a single probabilityP(A) = 0.30

General habits

  • Define events with letters before computing and write the target as a formula first (e.g. “want P(A′|B)”).
  • Use a table or tree whenever two or more categories are given; most percentages-of-policyholders questions are Venn problems.
  • Check reasonableness: probabilities lie in [0,1], and P(A∩B) can never exceed the smaller of P(A) and P(B).
  • Answer choices are often close; keep at least 4 decimal places until the final step.
  • If a problem has a dice, card or ball setup, listing or counting outcomes can beat a formula.

Common traps in General Probability

  1. Independent vs mutually exclusive. Exclusive means P(A∩B)=0; independent means P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B). With positive probabilities they cannot both hold.
  2. Double counting. P(A)+P(B) counts the overlap twice; subtract P(A∩B). With three events, add the triple back.
  3. Joint vs conditional wording. “20% of those under 40 lapse” is P(lapse | under 40), not P(lapse ∩ under 40). Multiply by P(under 40) to get the joint.
  4. Wrong denominator. After “given that B”, divide by P(B). Check that your answer is at least as big as the joint probability.
  5. Ordered vs unordered. Use C(n,r) in numerator and denominator for unordered draws, or P(n,r) in both for ordered. Never mix.
  6. Boundary words. “At most 1” means 0 or 1; “fewer than 2” is the same; “at least 2” is 1 − P(0 or 1).
  7. With or without replacement. Without replacement changes the later probabilities (use 3/9 not 4/10 for the second draw of the same kind).
  8. Assuming independence. Only use the product rule when the problem says so or when you can justify it.
  9. Early rounding. Carry 4+ decimals; the answer choices can be close together.
  10. Leaving a blank. Blanks score zero, the same as a wrong answer, so always pick a letter before time runs out.
Time triage for the real exam. About 6 minutes per question on average. On the first pass, answer anything you can finish in 3 to 4 minutes, flag the rest with your best guess recorded, and return to the flagged ones with the time you saved. Aim to finish the first pass in about 2 hours so you have close to an hour for the hard ones.
